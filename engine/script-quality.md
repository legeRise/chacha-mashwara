# Script Quality Gate

Every full episode script must pass this gate before production. It combines the
**flash-fiction** diagnostic (FF1–FF9) and the **endings** diagnostic (E1–E6).

The rule: **a script is not done because it has dialogue. It is done because it
passes the gate.**

---

## Pass 1 — Structure and frame

### FF1: Structure and pacing

- [ ] The first line hooks. It is not throat-clearing.
- [ ] Every beat does at least two jobs: plot + character, or plot + theme.
- [ ] There is a clear turn: the moment the rule is broken.
- [ ] The scope fits 50–65 seconds. If it needs a subplot, it is two episodes.
- [ ] The ending lands in the last 8 seconds, not after them.

### FF3: Beginning/ending frame

- [ ] The cold open promises a specific kind of story.
- [ ] The final image answers that promise visually.
- [ ] The verdict names the mistake, not the monster.
- [ ] The first and last images rhyme in some way (object, light, sound).

### E6: Ending pacing

- [ ] The climax is the twist, not the explanation.
- [ ] The return is short. No epilogue.
- [ ] The final image is held for two seconds and then cut.
- [ ] Nothing after the verdict except "Rule yaad rakhna" and silence.

---

## Pass 2 — Character, subtext, and logic

### FF2: Character compression

- [ ] The protagonist wants something specific in the first 5 seconds.
- [ ] Their reason for breaking the rule is sympathetic: love, guilt, fear,
      politeness, need, pride, or hope. Not curiosity.
- [ ] We know one concrete thing about their life (object, relationship, job,
      habit) without a backstory dump.
- [ ] The choice at the breach is in character.

### FF4: Subtext

- [ ] At least one important fact is implied, not stated.
- [ ] The story uses an object to carry the unspoken thing (card, plate, chain,
      photo, ring, bill).
- [ ] The entity offers the protagonist exactly what they want. That is the trap.
- [ ] The audience can infer the relationship without being told.

### FF9: Logical consistency

- [ ] Every character knows only what they could know at that moment.
- [ ] The timeline works. A character cannot be in two places.
- [ ] Phones, doors, locks, and locations behave consistently.
- [ ] If the supernatural breaks a rule, it breaks the same rule every time.
- [ ] The twist does not depend on a character acting stupidly.

---

## Pass 3 — Language, imagery, and theme

### FF5: Imagery

- [ ] There is one recurring visual motif (door, chain, mirror, card, water).
- [ ] The motif changes meaning by the end.
- [ ] The horror is shown through an ordinary object, not explained.
- [ ] No clichéd "dark figure with red eyes" language.

### FF6: Setting and senses

- [ ] The place is specific (a second-floor flat above a tailor's shop).
- [ ] At least two senses are engaged (sound, smell, touch, taste, sight).
- [ ] The setting reveals the character's state (untouched plate, empty chair).
- [ ] The corridor/outside is visually distinct from the safe room.

### FF7: Theme

- [ ] The theme is embodied in the choice, not stated in dialogue.
- [ ] The verdict complicates the theme; it does not explain it.
- [ ] There is no sermon. No character says what the story means.
- [ ] The ending preserves ambiguity about the entity, not about the event.

### FF8: Language and rhythm

- [ ] Verbs are precise. No "went," "did," "was" where a better verb exists.
- [ ] Dialogue sounds spoken, not written.
- [ ] Sentence lengths vary. Short lines land harder.
- [ ] The rule and verdict are the only lines allowed to sound "written."
- [ ] No line repeats information the audience already has.

---

## Endings gate (E1–E6)

- [ ] **E1 — Arbitrary:** The ending grows from the character's choice and the
      established rule. Not random.
- [ ] **E2 — Predictable:** The *what* can be familiar; the *how* must surprise.
- [ ] **E3 — Unearned:** The protagonist's choice causes the consequence. No luck,
      no new power, no convenient rescue.
- [ ] **E4 — Expanding:** The central question is answered. No new mystery in the
      last 5 seconds.
- [ ] **E5 — Overexplained:** No theme speech. No epilogue. End on image.
- [ ] **E6 — Pacing:** Climax, verdict, final image. Nothing else.

---

## The three-pass method

1. **Structural pass:** Cut anything that does not serve the rule, the breach, or
   the twist. If the story needs a second rule, split it.
2. **Character pass:** Give the protagonist one specific want and one specific
   object. Make the breach an emotional choice. Add subtext.
3. **Line pass:** Read every line aloud. Cut anything that sounds like writing. Make
   the verdict one sentence.

---

## Anti-patterns to reject

- **The miniature novel:** Too much backstory for 60 seconds.
- **The twist dependency:** The story only works because of the reveal; the first
  half is padding.
- **The vignette trap:** Beautiful images, no change.
- **The theme speech:** A character explains the moral.
- **The stupid breach:** The protagonist breaks the rule because they are an idiot,
  not because they are human.
- **The expanding ending:** A new mystery appears after the verdict.
- **The monster show:** The entity is fully shown and explained.

## Worked example: R-0001

The first draft had a generic young man, a generic reason ("he thought he recognized
her voice"), and a logical breach: Ayesha said she had been *knocking* for ten
minutes while she was at the wedding.

The gate changed it:

- **FF2:** Hamza is Ayesha's brother. He skipped her wedding after a fight. He wants
  to apologize.
- **FF4:** The wedding card and untouched plate carry the fight without exposition.
- **FF9:** Ayesha says she has been *calling* for ten minutes, not knocking. She is
  at the wedding; the location pin proves it.
- **E1/E3:** Hamza opens the door because he wants to repair the relationship. The
  Voice offers forgiveness. The consequence comes from his choice.
- **E5:** The verdict is one line: "Tum ne maafi ke liye darwaza khola. Usne ijazat
  samajh li." No explanation.
- **Final image:** The wedding card is now part of the image. The thing he wanted to
  fix is the thing left behind.

---

## Sign-off

A script is approved for production only when:

- [ ] It passes all three passes.
- [ ] It has one rule, one breach, one twist, one verdict.
- [ ] It fits 50–65 seconds at read-through speed.
- [ ] The final image is written as a shot, not a summary.
- [ ] The verdict is one sentence.
- [ ] No line explains the supernatural.
